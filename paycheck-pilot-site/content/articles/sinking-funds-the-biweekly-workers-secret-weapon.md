Every biweekly worker knows the same gut-punch: the month is going fine, and then the car needs new tires ($600), or Christmas shows up ($400), or the registration renewal lands ($150). None of these are surprises — you knew all year they were coming — but they still blow up the budget because the money was never set aside in advance.

That's what a sinking fund fixes. A **sinking fund** is just a pot of money you build up slowly for a specific expense you know is coming. You put a little in from every paycheck, and when the expense arrives, the money is already there. No credit card, no overdraft, no "I'll figure it out."

If you get paid every two weeks, sinking funds are even more powerful than they are for salaried workers, because you get 26 paychecks a year instead of 24 monthly ones. That extra rhythm — and the two 3-paycheck months — makes the math work in your favor.

## Why biweekly pay makes irregular expenses hurt more

A salaried worker gets the same amount on the same day every month, so a $600 tire bill is one month's problem. A biweekly worker gets paid on a rolling schedule: some months you get two checks, some months you get three, and the dates shift every month. A $600 bill that lands in a two-paycheck month, in the same week as rent, is a crisis — even if your annual income covers it easily.

The fix isn't earning more. It's **spreading the cost across time** so that no single paycheck has to carry a whole irregular expense. That's the entire idea behind a sinking fund: take a big, lumpy cost and turn it into a small, flat one.

## Step 1: List every irregular expense

Sit down once and write out every expense that isn't a monthly bill. Go through the last 12 months of bank statements if you can — that's where the real list lives, not in your memory. Most biweekly workers end up with something like this:

| Expense | Cost | How often |
|---|---|---|
| Car tires | $600 | Every 2 years |
| Car registration | $150 | Every year |
| Car maintenance (oil, brakes, fluids) | $500 | Every year |
| Christmas and gifts | $400 | Every year |
| Birthday gifts | $240 | Every year |
| Back-to-school costs | $200 | Every year |
| Home/renter's insurance deductible | $500 | If needed |
| Pet vet visit | $300 | Every year |
| Vacation or time off | $600 | Every year |

Your list will look different. The point is to catch the expenses that ambush you — the ones that aren't on your monthly bill list but absolutely happen every year.

## Step 2: Turn each expense into a per-paycheck amount

The formula is simple:

**Annual cost ÷ 26 paychecks = amount to set aside each payday**

Why 26? Because biweekly pay means 26 checks a year. Divide by 26, not 24, and each amount gets a little smaller. Here's the example list worked out:

| Expense | Annual cost | Per paycheck (÷ 26) |
|---|---|---|
| Car tires | $300 (half of $600 every 2 years) | $11.54 |
| Car registration | $150 | $5.77 |
| Car maintenance | $500 | $19.23 |
| Christmas and gifts | $400 | $15.38 |
| Birthday gifts | $240 | $9.23 |
| Back-to-school | $200 | $7.69 |
| Insurance deductible | $500 | $19.23 |
| Vet visit | $300 | $11.54 |
| Vacation | $600 | $23.08 |
| **Total** | **$3,190** | **$122.69** |

So about $123 per paycheck covers every irregular expense on the list. That's the real number: not $3,190 all at once, but $123 out of each check, before you spend anything else.

Notice the tires line: a $600 expense every two years is $300 a year, which is $11.54 per paycheck. People forget this step and save the full $600 in one year, then wonder why the fund is overfunded. Spread the cost over the actual cycle.

## Step 3: Set up the funds (one account, tracked separately)

You don't need ten bank accounts. You need **one separate savings account** and a way to track what each dollar in it is for. Options, simplest first:

1. **A spreadsheet or notebook.** One column per fund, updated on payday. Free, and it works.
2. **A {{< aff "amazon-planner" "budget planner" >}}.** Write each fund's target and current balance in the monthly pages. If you're a paper person, this is the version that actually gets used.
3. **A bank that lets you create sub-accounts or buckets.** Some banks and credit unions let you split one savings account into labeled buckets for free. If yours does, use it — the labels do the tracking for you.

On payday, move the total — in the example, $123 — into the savings account *before* anything else. Treat it like a bill. If you wait until the end of the pay period to move what's left, there won't be anything left.

## Step 4: Pay from the fund, then refill it

When the expense arrives, pay it from the sinking fund — not from checking, not from a credit card. Then keep funding at the same per-paycheck rate so the pot refills for next time.

**Example:** Your tires wear out in March. The fund holds $600 for tires because you've been putting in $11.54 a check for two years. You pay the shop $600 from the fund. The tire fund is now at $0 — but you keep putting $11.54 in every paycheck, and in two years it's $600 again, right when the next set is due.

This is the part people skip, and it's the part that matters most. A sinking fund you drain and never refill is just a savings account with a fancy name. The refill is automatic: same amount, every paycheck, whether or not the expense is near.

## Step 5: Use the 3-paycheck months to jump-start

Twice a year, biweekly pay produces a month with three paychecks instead of two. That third check is the best sinking-fund accelerator there is, because your monthly bills are already covered by the first two checks.

If your funds are starting from zero, put the entire third paycheck's bill money — or as much of it as you can — into the sinking funds. In the example above, one third paycheck of $1,400 could fund nearly half a year of sinking funds in one shot. After that, the regular $123 per paycheck just keeps everything topped up.

Even if you can't spare the whole third check, half of it gets you months ahead. The 3-paycheck month is the one time of year you can build a year's worth of irregular-expense coverage without feeling it.

## What if $123 per paycheck is too much right now?

Then start smaller — but start. Two rules:

1. **Fund the most urgent one first.** If your tires are bald and registration is due in two months, fund those two before Christmas. Rank by due date, not by size.
2. **Start with half.** $60 per paycheck covering the top three or four funds beats $0 covering nothing. Add a fund each time your income rises or a monthly bill drops off.

A half-funded sinking fund still turns a $600 emergency into a $300 problem, and a $300 problem is one you can solve without a credit card.

## The funds most biweekly workers forget

Three funds show up in almost every "I didn't see it coming" story:

- **Car maintenance beyond oil changes.** Tires, brakes, and batteries are not emergencies — they wear out on a schedule. If your car is over five years old, budget for them.
- **Medical and dental copays.** Even with insurance, a $35 copay three times a year and a $150 dental visit add up to real money.
- **The "annual bills" fund.** Some bills only arrive once a year — subscriptions, memberships, professional licenses, vehicle registration. If it's not monthly, it belongs in a sinking fund, not in your monthly budget.

## The full checklist

1. **List it.** Write down every irregular expense from the last 12 months of statements.
2. **Divide it.** Annual cost ÷ 26 paychecks = your per-paycheck amount for each fund.
3. **Separate it.** One savings account, tracked per fund, funded on payday before anything else.
4. **Spend from it.** When the expense arrives, pay from the fund — then keep refilling at the same rate.
5. **Boost it.** Use 3-paycheck months to jump-start new funds or top up thin ones.
6. **Start small if you must.** Half-funded beats unfunded, and the most urgent fund comes first.

That's it. No new app, no new bank account required, no willpower test at the end of the month. A sinking fund just moves the pain from the week the bill arrives to a dozen tiny paydays where you barely notice it — and the week the bill arrives stops being a crisis at all.
